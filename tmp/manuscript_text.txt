            CHAPTER 1 INTRODUCTION TO THE STUDY

      Background of the Study and Conceptual Framework


    Urinalysis remains one of the most fundamental and

widely utilized diagnostic procedures in primary

healthcare, serving as a rapid and cost-effective method

for detecting urinary tract infections (UTIs), renal

abnormalities, metabolic irregularities, and other systemic

conditions. Standard reagent dipsticks enable simultaneous

semi-quantitative assessment of multiple biochemical

parameters—including nitrite, leukocyte esterase, protein,

glucose, blood, ketones, pH, and specific gravity—within

minutes. These parameters provide clinically useful

indicators of urinary tract conditions and kidney-related

abnormalities. Due to its low cost and minimal

infrastructure requirements, dipstick urinalysis is

extensively used in community health centers and rural

primary care facilities.


    Despite its accessibility, dipstick interpretation in

many healthcare environments still relies on manual visual

comparison of reagent pad color changes with printed

reference charts. This process introduces variability

arising from ambient lighting conditions, reader

                        West Visayas State University
            COLLEGE OF INFORMATION AND COMMUNICATIONS TECHNOLOGY
                        La Paz, Iloilo City, Philippines

subjectivity, fatigue, and inconsistencies in timing during

reagent development. In resource-constrained healthcare

environments such as Philippine Rural Health Units (RHUs),

where automated reflectance photometry analyzers may be

unavailable due to financial and infrastructure

limitations, manual interpretation remains a common

workflow [1], [2]. Previous studies have documented

measurable inter-reader variability in visual dipstick

analysis [3], [4], particularly under inconsistent

illumination conditions such as variations in warm and cool

fluorescent lighting [5], [6]. These operational

constraints may reduce reproducibility and affect the

reliability of early disease screening in routine clinical

practice.


    Recent developments in mobile health (mHealth)

technologies have explored the use of smartphone cameras as

accessible tools for colorimetric analysis of urine reagent

strips. Controlled laboratory investigations have shown

that smartphone-based image analysis can achieve promising

classification performance across several urinalysis

parameters [5], [7]. However, a persistent technical

challenge in these systems involves sensitivity to

illumination conditions. Variations in color temperature,

light intensity, camera sensor characteristics, and

automatic exposure adjustments may introduce non-linear

color distortions that affect classification stability.

Many existing approaches assume controlled lighting

environments or rely on external calibration accessories,

which may limit their practicality in real-world rural or

community healthcare settings where illumination conditions

are often unpredictable.


    In addition to illumination variability, another

limitation observed in many smartphone-based urinalysis

systems is their focus on unimodal image classification.

These systems typically treat dipstick analysis purely as a

computer vision task without incorporating relevant

clinical context. In routine clinical practice, however,

urinalysis findings are rarely interpreted in isolation.

Healthcare workers commonly consider patient-reported

symptoms and relationships among multiple dipstick

parameters when evaluating potential conditions. For

example, the simultaneous presence of nitrite and leukocyte

esterase may increase the likelihood of bacterial urinary

tract infection, particularly when accompanied by symptoms

such as painful urination, urinary urgency, or fever [8],

diagnostic characteristics—for instance, nitrite tests

symptom information may provide a more structured basis for

preliminary risk assessment.


    These two operational challenges—(1) variability in

dipstick color interpretation under inconsistent

illumination and (2) the limited integration of symptom

information in automated urinalysis tools—highlight

opportunities for improving smartphone-assisted screening

systems. Addressing both aspects within a unified framework

may support healthcare workers in obtaining more consistent

dipstick interpretations while also providing structured

decision-support information during preliminary screening.


    In response to these challenges, this study proposes

the development of a smartphone-based Clinical Decision

Support System (CDSS) designed to assist urinalysis

screening in resource-constrained healthcare environments.

The system incorporates illumination normalization

techniques to improve the stability of smartphone-based

dipstick color classification under varying lighting

conditions. Image-based classification outputs are then

combined with structured symptom inputs using a

interpreting urinalysis findings. It does not replace

laboratory diagnostic procedures or professional medical

evaluation.

analysis with symptom-informed probabilistic reasoning,

this study aims to demonstrate the feasibility of a

context-appropriate mobile screening support system for

urinalysis in resource-limited healthcare settings.

                    Objectives of the Study



    This study aims to design, develop, and evaluate an

offline, edge-computing smartphone application for

automated urinalysis screening that integrates

illumination-adaptive computer vision with a multimodal

Bayesian Late Fusion framework for disease risk estimation.



Specifically, it aims to:


  1. Construct and develop a calibrated dataset of

    urinalysis reagent strip images using a combined

    dataset of volunteer urine specimens and standardized

    synthetic control solutions imaged under three

    controlled lighting conditions (2700K, 4000K, and

    5500K) for classifier training and algorithm

    calibration.

  2. Develop an Adaptive White Balance (AWB) normalization

    method that compensates for color temperature

    variations by referencing the unreacted white plastic

    region of the strip as a static calibration point.

  3. Develop an offline mobile application featuring an in-

    app automated Region of Interest (ROI) segmentation

    method using adaptive thresholding and contour

                    West Visayas State University
        COLLEGE OF INFORMATION AND COMMUNICATIONS TECHNOLOGY
                    La Paz, Iloilo City, Philippines

  detection to accurately isolate the ten reagent pads

  under variable backgrounds.

4. Train and evaluate a per-analyte k-Nearest Neighbors

  (k-NN) classification model on the constructed

  dataset—assessing training performance using accuracy,

  Kappa per analyte parameter—and integrate this visual

  classifier into a Bayesian Late Fusion framework to

  estimate posterior risk probabilities for urinary

5. Evaluate the application’s usability, functionality,

                       Definition of Terms


    To ensure clarity and uniformity in the interpretation

of this study, the following terms are operationally

defined based on how they are utilized within the proposed

system:


Adaptive White-Balance (AWB) In this study, Adaptive White

Balance refers to the image preprocessing technique used to

reduce the effects of color temperature variation in

smartphone-captured images. Operationally, the system

applies a reference-based gray world assumption by

detecting the unreacted white plastic region of the urine

reagent strip and using it as a calibration reference. This

normalization step adjusts the color balance of the

captured image to minimize lighting-induced color shifts

before further color feature extraction is performed.


Bayesian Late Fusion Model In the context of this study,

this term refers to the probabilistic reasoning component

used to estimate disease risk by integrating outputs from

dipstick color classification with structured symptom

inputs. The system applies Bayesian inference to combine

visual classification probabilities and symptom-based

likelihoods with predefined prior probabilities for



                                    18
                      West Visayas State University
          COLLEGE OF INFORMATION AND COMMUNICATIONS TECHNOLOGY
                      La Paz, Iloilo City, Philippines

selected urinary conditions. This probabilistic aggregation

produces posterior risk estimates intended to support

preliminary screening and does not represent a definitive

medical diagnosis.


Clinical Decision Support System (CDSS) In the context of

this research, CDSS refers to the offline Android

smartphone application being developed. It is operationally

defined as a presumptive screening and triage support tool

designed to assist Rural Health Unit (RHU) personnel and

guided users, and not as a definitive diagnostic

replacement for laboratory analyzers or physicians.


Control Solutions (Synthetic Urine) This refers to

commercially prepared, in vitro standardized liquids with

manufacturer-specified biochemical concentration ranges

(e.g., Level 1 Negative and Level 2 Positive controls).

Operationally, these solutions are used in place of real

human urine during the initial dataset development phase to

establish controlled reference labels for algorithm

calibration.


Edge Computing This refers to the software architecture of

the proposed system. Operationally, it means that all image

processing, machine learning classification, and



                                    19
                      West Visayas State University
          COLLEGE OF INFORMATION AND COMMUNICATIONS TECHNOLOGY
                      La Paz, Iloilo City, Philippines

probabilistic reasoning are executed locally on the

smartphone’s internal processor (CPU), requiring no

reliance on cloud servers, external databases, or active

internet connectivity.


HSV Color Space (Hue, Saturation, Value) Unlike the

standard RGB (Red, Green, Blue) format, HSV is the

mathematical color representation used by the system to

quantify dipstick pad colors. Operationally, separating Hue

and Saturation from Value reduces dependence on brightness

intensity compared to raw RGB analysis, improving

robustness under varying lighting conditions.


Illumination-Invariant In this study, this term refers to

the system’s engineered ability to reduce classification

variability caused by lighting-induced color shifts.

Operationally, it is evaluated based on maintaining

consistent classification performance across predefined

lighting conditions (2700K, 4000K, and 5500K) within

acceptable variance thresholds.


k-Nearest Neighbors (k-NN) This is the supervised machine

learning algorithm used as the system’s visual classifier.

Operationally, it plots extracted HSV feature values of a

scanned dipstick pad within a multidimensional feature

                      West Visayas State University
          COLLEGE OF INFORMATION AND COMMUNICATIONS TECHNOLOGY
                      La Paz, Iloilo City, Philippines

space, computes the distance to the nearest labeled data

points in the reference dataset, and outputs a class

prediction, with probability estimated from the proportion

of nearest neighbors belonging to each class.


Multimodal Classification This refers to the system’s

Operationally, it combines continuous visual features (HSV

categorical clinical metadata (e.g., Yes/No symptom inputs

disease risk estimation.


Reagent Dipstick (10-Parameter) This refers to the physical

diagnostic tool used in the study. Operationally, it is a

commercially available plastic strip containing ten

chemically treated pads (e.g., Leukocytes, Nitrite,

Urobilinogen, Protein, pH, Blood, Specific Gravity, Ketone,

Bilirubin, Glucose) that undergo color changes upon

exposure to synthetic control solutions or urine samples.


Region of Interest (ROI) Segmentation This refers to the

analysis. Operationally, it uses adaptive thresholding and

contour detection to locate the dipstick in the captured

Current Systems

Manual Urinalysis Workflow in Philippine Rural Health Units


    In Philippine Rural Health Units (RHUs), routine

urinalysis is primarily performed using standard 10-

parameter reagent dipsticks such as Multistix 10 SG, SD

UroColor, or similar cost-effective alternatives, and

results are interpreted by manual visual comparison against

manufacturer-provided reference color charts [[1]. This

workflow remains the dominant diagnostic approach in

primary care settings due to limited access to laboratory

automation, budget constraints, and infrastructure

limitations [2], [3]. These constraints often necessitate

the use of shelf-stable, manual point-of-care testing

(POCT) over more complex automated systems [4].

    The current diagnostic landscape reveals a structural

imbalance. Rural facilities depend on subjective manual

interpretation methods vulnerable to environmental

illumination shifts, reagent timing variability, and

operator-dependent inconsistency. Conversely, urban

infrastructural investment.

Illumination Calibration


    Color Constancy and the Gray World Assumption


    The digital interpretation of colorimetric diagnostics

is fundamentally challenged by the variability of ambient

illumination. The human visual system overcomes this

challenge through color constancy, a perceptual mechanism

by which consistent colors are perceived despite shifts in

the illumination spectrum [26]. The computational analog to

this mechanism is the Gray World Assumption, originally

formalized by Buchsbaum [27] and comprehensively surveyed

by Gijsenij et al. [28], which posits that the average


                                    38
                      West Visayas State University
          COLLEGE OF INFORMATION AND COMMUNICATIONS TECHNOLOGY
                      La Paz, Iloilo City, Philippines

reflectance of a scene approximates a neutral gray. While

these global statistical assumptions are foundational,

recent advancements by Yan et al. [29] have refined this

principle by shifting from global scene averages to local

reflectance differences. This modern framework demonstrates

that traditional assumptions like Gray World remain

technically viable in complex, multi-illuminant

environments when integrated into hybrid models that

account for local spatial variations [29].


    The proposed system incorporates a reference-based

modification of this assumption. Rather than computing the

global scene mean, the mean intensity calculation is

restricted to a known "True White" reference region of the

dipstick pad. This targeted approach achieves more reliable

chromatic adaptation, as it isolates the reference from the

chromatic content of the reagent pads themselves [19]. The

approach is consistent with findings by Finlayson and

Trezzi [30], who demonstrated that spatially restricted

gray-world computations outperform global averaging when

the scene contains dominant chromatic regions. This

principle has been further refined in recent region-

weighted frameworks, such as that proposed by Wang et al.

[31], which utilizes multi-scale semantic guidance to



                                    39
                      West Visayas State University
          COLLEGE OF INFORMATION AND COMMUNICATIONS TECHNOLOGY
                      La Paz, Iloilo City, Philippines

suppress the influence of biased chromatic regions and

prioritize locally stable neutral patches for illuminant

estimation.




    Von Kries Chromatic Adaptation and Gain Scaling


    The gain-scaling transformation applied by the

Illumination Calibrator is grounded in the Von Kries

Diagonal Model of Chromatic Adaptation, as formalized by

the International Commission on Illumination [26]. This

model posits that chromatic adaptation can be achieved by

independently scaling the gain of each primary sensor

channel (R, G, and B) through a diagonal matrix

transformation, rather than through full-rank matrix

inversion. Each channel gain is defined as the ratio

between a global target intensity and the measured mean

intensity of the corresponding channel within the reference

region.


    This formulation is computationally advantageous

because diagonal scaling requires only three scalar

multiplications per pixel, making it well-suited for pixel-

wise transformation on mobile central processing units

(CPUs) operating under battery and thermal constraints


                                    40
                      West Visayas State University
          COLLEGE OF INFORMATION AND COMMUNICATIONS TECHNOLOGY
                      La Paz, Iloilo City, Philippines

[19]. The model operates on the assumption that the sensor

channels respond independently to illumination changes, a

condition that holds sufficiently well for consumer-grade

mobile camera sensors under the illumination range of 2700K

to 5500K targeted by this system.


    Several illumination correction strategies have been

evaluated for suitability in attachment-free smartphone

colorimetric diagnostics. Willmott et al. (2020)

demonstrated that ambient subtraction using flash/no-flash

image pairs achieves device-independent colorimetric

consistency, but this approach requires two sequential

captures and custom per-device calibration, creating

workflow friction in point-of-care settings [32]. Machine

learning-based color constancy approaches achieve state-of-

the-art performance on natural scenes but introduce non-

transparent transformations, require pre-training on large

datasets, and impose computational overhead incompatible

with offline edge deployment [33].


    The reference-based white balance modification adopted

in this study avoids these failure modes by restricting the

illuminant estimate to the physically known neutral plastic

substrate of the dipstick, rather than inferring it from

unconstrained scene statistics. This spatially restricted


                                    41
                      West Visayas State University
          COLLEGE OF INFORMATION AND COMMUNICATIONS TECHNOLOGY
                      La Paz, Iloilo City, Philippines

reference approach is consistent with the color correction

principle validated by Balbach et al. (2021), who confirmed

that embedding a known-neutral reference within the image

provides reproducible illumination compensation across

diverse smartphone hardware and ambient lighting conditions

without requiring hardware attachments [19].

Segmentation


Static Geometry Mapping over Deep Learning Object Detection


    Image segmentation in point-of-care diagnostics

requires accurate localization of reagent pad regions of

interest (ROIs) without imposing prohibitive computational

demands on the host device. Contemporary object detection

frameworks, such as You Only Look Once (YOLO) and its

derivatives, achieve high generalization across diverse

visual scenes but require substantial computational

resources, including floating-point operations, GPU



                                    43
                      West Visayas State University
          COLLEGE OF INFORMATION AND COMMUNICATIONS TECHNOLOGY
                      La Paz, Iloilo City, Philippines

acceleration, and significant memory bandwidth [34]. These

requirements are incompatible with the processing capacity

and battery life of budget-specification Android devices

prevalent in resource-constrained Philippine RHU

deployments.


    As an alternative, the proposed system employs static

geometry mapping—a technique grounded in the prior

structural knowledge of the dipstick's fixed physical

layout. This approach is consistent with the segmentation

methodology employed by Ra et al., who demonstrated that

geometric constraint-based ROI partitioning—wherein the

center of each reagent pad region is computed from fixed

structural coordinates and ROI boundaries are derived from

those centroids—achieves reliable pad localization for

standardized ten-parameter urine test strips while

remaining computationally feasible on mobile platforms

[22]. By exploiting the invariant geometry of the dipstick

rather than learning it from data, the system eliminates

the need for a large annotated segmentation dataset and

reduces the risk of localization failure in low-texture or

high-glare capture conditions [35].

    The segmentation algorithm divides a vertically

oriented dipstick cutout into exactly ten equally spaced

slots corresponding to the ten reagent pads of a standard

ten-parameter urinalysis strip. This grid slicing approach

is based on the calculated usable height of the image

region, from which uniform slot boundaries are derived

through linear partitioning. The center of each slot is

then localized using the midpoint formula, yielding the

centroid coordinates used for color sampling in the

subsequent Feature Extraction module.


    This method is consistent with prior implementations

of smartphone-based dipstick readers, which have

demonstrated that fixed-geometry partitioning achieves

reliable pad localization when image capture is guided by

an on-screen alignment overlay [36]. The reliability of

this approach is contingent on controlled capture

alignment, which the system enforces through the user

interface.


Feature Extraction

Cylindrical Color Modeling and HSV Color Space Conversion


    The feature extraction module transforms the sampled

pixel data from each reagent pad ROI into numerical feature



                                       45
                      West Visayas State University
          COLLEGE OF INFORMATION AND COMMUNICATIONS TECHNOLOGY
                      La Paz, Iloilo City, Philippines

vectors suitable for machine learning classification. This

transformation begins with a color space conversion from

the Red-Green-Blue (RGB) representation produced by the

camera sensor to the Hue-Saturation-Value (HSV) cylindrical

color model.


    The selection of the HSV color space for clinical

colorimetry is supported by its capacity to decouple

chromatic information from luminance. In the RGB model, all

three channels are simultaneously affected by changes in

illumination intensity, making it difficult to isolate

chemically induced color changes from environmental

interference [37]. This limitation is particularly

consequential in multi-analyte strip analysis, where subtle

pad-to-pad colorimetric differences must be resolved

independently [38]. In contrast, the HSV model encodes

chromatic information primarily in the Hue channel, which

remains relatively invariant to changes in illumination

intensity and surface shadow [19]. This property is

clinically significant because the colorimetric reactions

on urinalysis reagent pads produce shifts in hue—such as

yellow-to-green in protein detection or tan-to-purple in

leukocyte esterase detection—that are more reliably

captured by the Hue channel than by any single RGB



                                    46
                      West Visayas State University
          COLLEGE OF INFORMATION AND COMMUNICATIONS TECHNOLOGY
                      La Paz, Iloilo City, Philippines

component.While perceptually uniform color spaces such as

CIELAB offer superior colorimetric precision and are

considered the gold standard in quantitative colorimetry,

HSV was selected for this system due to its native support

in the OpenCV for Android library and its lower

computational overhead, which is critical for real-time

processing on entry-level mobile hardware [39].


Statistical Averaging and Variance Reduction


    Each reagent pad ROI contains a distribution of pixel

values that reflects both the underlying chemical reaction

and sensor-level noise sources, including photon shot noise

and thermal noise. To produce a stable feature value from

this distribution, the system computes the mean Hue (μH)

and mean Saturation (μS) across all pixels within the ROI.

This statistical averaging approach implements a variance

reduction technique established in digital image

processing, whereby the mean of a large pixel sample

converges toward the true signal value as individual noise

contributions cancel out [37].


    The incorporation of the Saturation channel alongside

Hue is supported by Yang et al., who demonstrated through

RGB-to-CIELab color space transformation on commercial



                                    47
                      West Visayas State University
          COLLEGE OF INFORMATION AND COMMUNICATIONS TECHNOLOGY
                      La Paz, Iloilo City, Philippines

urinalysis strips that human-vision-based models capture

subtle colorimetric shifts in low-concentration analytes—

including glucose and protein—more effectively than

luminance-weighted RGB models, enabling high accuracy in

point-of-care settings [40].


Integrative Evidence Analysis


    Pathophysiology and Clinical Correlates


    The classification logic of the integrative analysis

module is grounded in the established pathophysiology of

the three target conditions screened by the system.


    Urinary Tract Infection (UTI) is primarily

characterized by the colonization of the urinary tract by

pathogenic bacteria, most commonly Escherichia coli. This

triggers an inflammatory response in the urothelium,

resulting in the recruitment of neutrophils (pyuria) and

occasional micro-hematuria [7]. The system's visual

evidence stream for UTI incorporates the Griess Reaction,

in which bacterial nitrate reductase converts dietary

nitrate to nitrite, producing a detectable color change on

the nitrite reagent pad. The leukocyte esterase (LE) pad

simultaneously detects the enzymatic activity of

infiltrating neutrophils. Clinical symptoms such as dysuria


                                    48
                      West Visayas State University
          COLLEGE OF INFORMATION AND COMMUNICATIONS TECHNOLOGY
                      La Paz, Iloilo City, Philippines

and urinary frequency serve as corroborating evidence in

the system's probabilistic framework [41].


    Renal conditions, including Chronic Kidney Disease

(CKD) and Glomerulonephritis, involve structural damage to

the glomerular filtration barrier, which permits the

pathological passage of albumin into the urine [42]. The

detection of proteinuria in the proposed system is based on

the protein error of indicators principle: the reagent pad

contains tetrabromophenol blue buffered at pH ≈ 3.0, which

shifts from yellow to green or blue in the presence of

albumin due to the release of hydrogen ions [8]. Clinical

symptoms such as peripheral edema and fatigue are

integrated into the system's weighting logic to flag

persistent proteinuria for urgent renal triage.


k-Nearest Neighbors Classification


    The visual classification component of the Late Fusion

Analysis module is implemented using the k-Nearest

Neighbors (k-NN) algorithm. k-NN is a non-parametric,

instance-based learning method that classifies a query

sample by identifying the k most similar examples in a

labeled reference dataset and assigning the majority class

among those neighbors [43], [44]. Similarity is measured

                      West Visayas State University
          COLLEGE OF INFORMATION AND COMMUNICATIONS TECHNOLOGY
                      La Paz, Iloilo City, Philippines

using the Euclidean distance between the query's feature

vector and each reference vector in the two-dimensional

(μH, μS) space. The validity of the Euclidean metric in

this context is contingent on isotropic feature scaling, a

condition satisfied by normalizing the HSV feature vectors

prior to classification.


    The k-NN algorithm was selected over parametric

alternatives—such as logistic regression or naïve bayes

classifiers and over deep learning architectures for two

reasons. First, k-NN does not require a large training

dataset to define a decision boundary, making it suitable

for the moderate sample sizes attainable in a Philippine

RHU clinical validation study [45]. Second, k-NN provides

instance-based explanations by revealing which specific

training neighbors contributed to a given prediction, a

core principle of Explainable AI (XAI) that is discussed

further [46].


    The performance of k-NN is governed by the bias-

variance tradeoff: small values of k produce low-bias but

high-variance decision boundaries sensitive to noise, while

large values of k produce smoother boundaries at the cost

of increased bias. The selection of an optimal k is

                       West Visayas State University
          COLLEGE OF INFORMATION AND COMMUNICATIONS TECHNOLOGY

addressed empirically through cross-validation in the

system's evaluation phase [43].


    The selection of k-NN over alternative supervised

classifiers was informed by the specific constraints of

this study's deployment context. Kim et al. (2025)

    Support Vector Machines offer strong generalization by

larger training sets for reliable ensemble variance.

    Bayesian Fusion and Clinical Reasoning


    Relying exclusively on the k-NN visual classifier

would render the system a digital color reader—a tool that

assigns diagnostic classes based solely on the mathematical

similarity of HSV vectors to reference data, without

accounting for clinical symptomatology. Such a unimodal

approach is clinically inadequate because it treats visual

results (continuous colorimetric data) and patient symptoms

(categorical clinical evidence) as equivalent coordinates

in a mathematical space, failing to represent their

differing diagnostic specificity and sensitivity [49].


    Clinical diagnosis is inherently a probabilistic,

iterative process where every piece of evidence—whether a

chemical biomarker or a physical symptom—updates the

posterior probability of a diagnosis. To implement this

effectively in a computational environment, the system must

utilize a structured information fusion architecture [50].


    Information fusion architectures are broadly

classified into three paradigms based on the stage at which

different data streams are integrated [51].


    Early Fusion (Feature-Level): This approach

concatenates raw features (e.g., continuous HSV vectors and


                                    54
                      West Visayas State University
          COLLEGE OF INFORMATION AND COMMUNICATIONS TECHNOLOGY
                      La Paz, Iloilo City, Philippines

binary categorical symptoms) into a single feature space

before classification. While it allows for early

correlation detection, it is unsuitable here because

combining geometrically distinct data types produces

"meaningless distances" in the feature space, reducing

classifier reliability [51].


    Intermediate Fusion (Representation-Level): Modality-

specific representations are merged at hidden layers,

typically within deep learning models. While powerful for

cross-modal interactions, this "black box" approach is

incompatible with Explainable AI (XAI) requirements, as

users cannot trace per-modality contributions [49].


    Late Fusion (Decision-Level): Each data stream

produces independent outputs combined at the final decision

stage. This is optimal for heterogeneous inputs like visual

and symptom data, preserving interpretability while

handling complementary errors [52].


    The proposed system utilizes a Bayesian Late-Fusion

architecture, treating the k-NN classification result as

probabilistic visual evidence that is subsequently updated

by patient-reported symptoms via Bayesian inference [50].

This dual-stream integration addresses the inherent



                                    55
                        West Visayas State University
            COLLEGE OF INFORMATION AND COMMUNICATIONS TECHNOLOGY
                        La Paz, Iloilo City, Philippines

limitations of chemical reagent strips; for instance,

clinical symptoms like dysuria serve as a weighting factor

to increase the posterior probability of a UTI, effectively

mitigating false-negative results from nitrite-negative

Gram-positive infections or inadequate bladder incubation

[53]


       To ensure clinical relevance, baseline "prior

probabilities" are anchored to Philippine epidemiological

data. The system integrates hospital morbidity rates from

the DOH Field Health Services Information System (FHSIS) to

establish a UTI prevalence baseline[54]. Furthermore, renal

risk estimates are informed by Philippine Society of

Nephrology data, which indicates that CKD affects

approximately 11.2% of the national population .[55]

Grounding the framework in these local statistics prevents

the over-diagnosis risks associated with generic

international datasets.


       By separating visual perception from probabilistic

clinical reasoning, the system functions as a transparent

Clinical Decision Support System (CDSS). This architecture

ensures that any risk classification can be traced back to

its individual source—whether the dipstick image, reported

symptoms, or both—upholding the Explainable AI (XAI)

principles required for deployment in primary care settings

Validation, Explainability, and Ethics

Diagnostic Accuracy and Cross-Validation


    The statistical reliability of the proposed system is

governed by established diagnostic accuracy study

protocols. Validating a point-of-care tool requires direct

comparison against a laboratory gold standard—in this case,

automated benchtop urinalysis or urine culture. The primary

performance metrics reported are sensitivity, specificity,

Precision, Recall, and the F1-score, derived from a

confusion matrix analysis [56]. While overall accuracy

provides a general overview, it is a misleading metric in

imbalanced medical datasets where the prevalence of

positive cases is low. The F1-score, which harmonizes

Precision and Recall, is therefore the primary metric for

evaluating per-condition diagnostic performance.


    To mitigate overfitting in small to moderate sample

sizes, the study employs stratified k-fold cross-

validation, reporting performance across multiple folds

rather than a single train-test split. This approach

reduces variance in estimated sensitivity and specificity




                                    57
                      West Visayas State University
          COLLEGE OF INFORMATION AND COMMUNICATIONS TECHNOLOGY
                      La Paz, Iloilo City, Philippines

and aligns with best practices in medical machine learning

validation [45], [57]. The minimum sample size for each

condition is determined following the method described by

Hajian-Tilaki, which derives sample size estimates from

target sensitivity and specificity values with specified

confidence intervals [45]. The Receiver Operating

Characteristic (ROC) Curve and the Area Under the Curve

(AUC) are additionally reported to characterize system

performance across varying decision thresholds [56].


    Explainable AI and Transparent Decision-Making


    In the deployment of intelligent systems within

healthcare, the tradeoff between model complexity and

interpretability is a critical ethical and practical

consideration [46]. This study incorporates Explainable AI

(XAI) principles—as taxonomized by Barredo Arrieta et al.—

by selecting k-NN over deep learning architectures [46].

This instance-based transparency fosters clinician trust

and ensures that the system functions as a decision support

tool rather than an autonomous diagnostic authority,

consistent with the CDSS design principles described by

Shortliffe and Cimino [50].

Protocol Justification

2 minutes are considered to have no diagnostic significance

Strip used in this study [64]. These timepoints indicate

the earliest and latest intervals during which each reagent

pad’s color change is considered valid for visual

interpretation.

interpretation of these timing windows differs slightly.

The recommended reading windows indicate when the color

reaction has sufficiently developed for interpretation;

changes immediately afterward. For most parameters, the

remains relatively consistent throughout the 120-second

characteristic of dry reagent pad chemistry: once the

reaction reaches completion, the resulting color typically

remains stable within the normal testing timeframe [8].

captured after sufficient reaction time to evaluate the

dipped. By this time, all ten parameters have had

capture multiple images at different time points, which may

be impractical in busy Rural Health Unit (RHU) settings.

Furthermore, it mirrors the operational principle of

automated urinalysis analyzers, which internally read each

pad at its optimal reaction time before producing a single

consolidated result [65]. The difference in this

implementation is that a smartphone camera is used instead

of an integrated optical sensor.

Methods and Proposed Enhancements


    The methodology of this study is architecturally

grounded in a Late Fusion framework that integrates two

functionally distinct computational modules: an

illumination-adaptive computer vision pipeline and a

probabilistic clinical reasoning engine. This hybrid

structure directly addresses the two principal limitations

identified in Chapter 1: (1) the sensitivity of smartphone-

based colorimetric analysis to ambient lighting

variability, and (2) the diagnostic limitations of unimodal

image classification systems that interpret reagent pad

color changes without incorporating symptom-informed

clinical context.


    The computer vision module is responsible for image

acquisition, illumination-adaptive preprocessing, reagent

pad segmentation, HSV-based color feature extraction, and

k-Nearest Neighbors (k-NN) visual classification. The

probabilistic reasoning module receives the k-NN

classification outputs together with structured patient

symptom inputs and applies Bayesian inference to compute

posterior disease risk probabilities.




                                    71
                      West Visayas State University
          COLLEGE OF INFORMATION AND COMMUNICATIONS TECHNOLOGY
                      La Paz, Iloilo City, Philippines

    A Late Fusion strategy is adopted, wherein modality-

specific outputs are independently computed before

probabilistic integration. This design preserves the

interpretability of each computational layer, maintains

computational efficiency suitable for on-device inference,

and avoids the instability that may arise from early fusion

of heterogeneous feature types — continuous colorimetric

vectors and categorical symptom variables — within a single

feature space.


    To achieve a comprehensive 10-parameter urinalysis

profile (Leukocytes, Nitrite, Urobilinogen, Protein, pH,

Blood, Specific Gravity, Ketone, Bilirubin, and Glucose),

the system utilizes a Multi-Class Feature Mapping approach.

vectors derived from 175 volunteer urine specimens, each

imaged under three controlled lighting conditions. This

dataset provides sufficient colorimetric coverage across

the four target analytes and their semi-quantitative grade

within the predefined operational range.

System Architecture Overview


    The proposed system follows a sequential five-stage

pipeline architecture implemented entirely on-device.


Stage 1 – Image Acquisition:


    The smartphone camera captures an image of the reacted

reagent dipstick under ambient indoor lighting conditions

within the predefined operational range (2700K–5500K). The

app supports both live camera capture via CameraController

and gallery selection via ImagePicker.



                                    73
                      West Visayas State University
          COLLEGE OF INFORMATION AND COMMUNICATIONS TECHNOLOGY
                      La Paz, Iloilo City, Philippines

Stage 2 – Illumination-Adaptive Preprocessing (AWB):


    An Adaptive White-Balance (AWB) algorithm performs

reference-based color normalization using the unreacted

white plastic substrate of the dipstick as a calibration

anchor. This step reduces color temperature–induced bias

prior to feature extraction, while acknowledging that

residual lighting variability may persist.


Stage 3 – Region of Interest (ROI) Segmentation:


    Automated segmentation isolates the ten chemically

treated reagent pads from the background using flood-fill

connected component analysis (_floodDarkComponent), with

pad positions placed geometrically via _findBestPadGrid and

_refinePadY relative to the detected marker. Fully

automatic — no user interaction required. This step ensures

that subsequent color analysis is restricted to the

relevant reaction regions and not influenced by extraneous

background pixels.


Stage 4 – HSV Feature Extraction and k-NN Classification:


    For each isolated pad, mean HSV feature vectors are

computed and submitted to the k-NN classifier. The

classifier returns both a class label and a neighbor-



                                    74
                      West Visayas State University
          COLLEGE OF INFORMATION AND COMMUNICATIONS TECHNOLOGY
                      La Paz, Iloilo City, Philippines

proportion probability, representing a soft confidence

estimate of the pad’s semi-quantitative reaction level.


Stage 5 – Bayesian Late Fusion and Risk Stratification:


    The Bayesian reasoning module integrates the per-pad

visual probabilities with structured clinical symptom

inputs to compute posterior probabilities for the

predefined screening categories: urinary tract infection

(UTI) and selected renal risk indicators. These posterior

probabilities are subsequently mapped to discrete risk

stratification levels presented to the user.


    All five stages are executed sequentially and locally

on the device, without reliance on external servers, cloud-

based processing, or network connectivity. This pipeline

represents the complete operational workflow of the CDSS,

from image capture to preliminary risk output.

Region of Interest (ROI) Segmentation


    Following illumination-adaptive preprocessing, the

system performs automated Region of Interest (ROI)

segmentation to isolate the pixel regions corresponding to

each of the ten reagent pads from the surrounding

                       West Visayas State University
          COLLEGE OF INFORMATION AND COMMUNICATIONS TECHNOLOGY
                      La Paz, Iloilo City, Philippines

background. This step is essential because variations in

camera distance, strip placement, orientation, and

background texture may otherwise introduce irrelevant pixel

data into the feature extraction process. Direct full-image

color analysis would therefore reduce classification

reliability by incorporating non-reactive regions.


    ROI segmentation is implemented through a structured

two-stage procedure.


Stage 1 – Dipstick Localization:


    Adaptive thresholding is applied to the color-

corrected image to generate a binary mask that

differentiates the dipstick structure from the background.

Unlike global thresholding, adaptive thresholding computes

local intensity thresholds across image neighborhoods,

allowing improved separation under uneven lighting

conditions. This produces a preliminary binary segmentation

highlighting the elongated strip geometry.


    Subsequently, contour detection is performed on the

thresholded mask to identify candidate geometric

boundaries. The contour corresponding to the dipstick strip

is selected based on geometric constraints such as area,

aspect ratio, and elongated rectangular structure. This


                                    85
                      West Visayas State University
          COLLEGE OF INFORMATION AND COMMUNICATIONS TECHNOLOGY
                      La Paz, Iloilo City, Philippines

step establishes the spatial boundary of the strip within

the image frame.


Stage 2 – Pad Region Extraction:


 Once the strip boundary is identified, the image is

optionally aligned or normalized relative to the detected

orientation to reduce rotational variability. The ten

reagent pads are then extracted as fixed relative sub-

regions along the longitudinal axis of the strip based on

the known physical layout of the standard 10-parameter

dipstick configuration.


    Each pad region is defined using proportional

positional offsets within the detected strip boundary

rather than absolute pixel coordinates, ensuring robustness

to moderate variations in scale and framing. The segmented

pad regions are then individually forwarded to the HSV

feature extraction stage for colorimetric analysis.


    This ROI segmentation strategy constrains subsequent

feature extraction strictly to chemically reactive areas of

interest, thereby improving classification consistency and

minimizing background-induced noise.

                      West Visayas State University
          COLLEGE OF INFORMATION AND COMMUNICATIONS TECHNOLOGY
                      La Paz, Iloilo City, Philippines

    Color features are extracted from each segmented

reagent pad in the HSV (Hue, Saturation, Value) color space

rather than the standard RGB color space. This

representation is selected because the Hue and Saturation

channels encode the chromatic properties of the pad color

change—which correspond to the chemical reaction of

interest—while the Value channel encodes brightness

intensity, which is more susceptible to residual

illumination variation. By operating primarily on Hue and

Saturation, the classifier focuses on the chemical signal

while reducing sensitivity to brightness-related noise that

the AWB correction may not have fully eliminated.


    For each segmented pad region, the mean Hue, mean

Saturation, and mean Value are computed across all pixels

within the region, yielding a three-dimensional HSV feature

vector x=[H,S,V]\mathbf{x} = [H, S, V] x=[H,S,V] per pad.

These feature vectors serve as the input to the k-NN

classifier in the subsequent stage.


Dual-Stage Classifier: k-Nearest Neighbors (k-NN) and

Bayesian Reasoning


    The classification architecture is structured as a

deliberate dual-stage design that separates the perceptual



                                    87
                      West Visayas State University
          COLLEGE OF INFORMATION AND COMMUNICATIONS TECHNOLOGY
                      La Paz, Iloilo City, Philippines

task of colorimetric pattern recognition from the

inferential task of disease-level probabilistic reasoning.

This separation is methodologically necessary because k-

Nearest Neighbors (k-NN), operating in HSV feature space,

is well suited to identifying geometric proximity between

an observed pad color and calibrated reference color

clusters, but it does not inherently account for the

asymmetric diagnostic importance of individual dipstick

parameters nor allow principled integration of

heterogeneous symptom variables. The Bayesian model in

Stage 2 explicitly addresses these limitations.


Stage 1 — k-Nearest Neighbors (k-NN) Visual Classification


    The k-NN algorithm functions as the system's

Perception Layer, operating on HSV feature vectors

extracted from each segmented reagent pad.


    Because the HSV color space is cylindrical, utilizing

a standard Euclidean distance metric would mathematically

misrepresent the Hue channel (where 0° and 360° both

represent red). To resolve this geometric inconsistency,

the k-NN algorithm employs a Circular Distance Metric

specifically for the Hue dimension. The angular distance

between any two hue vectors (H_1 and H_2) is calculated



                                    88
                         West Visayas State University
             COLLEGE OF INFORMATION AND COMMUNICATIONS TECHNOLOGY
                         La Paz, Iloilo City, Philippines

using the shortest path around the cylinder: d(H_1, H_2) =

min(|H_1 - H_2|, 360 - |H_1 - H_2|). This custom distance

metric prevents the classifier from incorrectly

differentiating between visually identical chromatic ends,

ensuring accurate colorimetric matching against the

reference dataset


    The k nearest neighbors are identified based on

smallest distance values. The predicted class for the pad

is determined through majority voting among these

neighbors.


    Rather than producing a hard classification only, the

model computes a soft visual probability estimate:


P_visual(Cⱼ | x) = (Number of neighbors belonging to class

Cⱼ) / k


    This neighbor-proportion probability represents the

visual confidence associated with each semi-quantitative

reaction level. These per-pad visual probabilities are then

forwarded to the Bayesian Late Fusion module.


    The value of k is selected through cross-validation on

the training dataset to balance sensitivity to subtle color

                         West Visayas State University
             COLLEGE OF INFORMATION AND COMMUNICATIONS TECHNOLOGY
                         La Paz, Iloilo City, Philippines

differences against robustness to noise and lighting

residuals.


Stage 2 — Bayesian Late Fusion for Probabilistic Disease

Risk Estimation


       The Bayesian model constitutes the system’s Reasoning

Layer. While k-NN operates independently per pad in

geometric feature space, the Bayesian model operates at the

disease level, integrating:


• Multi-parameter visual outputs

 • Symptom inputs

 • Prior disease prevalence

       The posterior probability of disease condition H given

evidence E is computed using Bayes’ Theorem:


P(H | E) = [P(E | H) × P(H)] / P(E)


Prior Probability — P(H): The prior probability represents

the baseline prevalence of the condition within the target

screening population before observing test results.


For:


 • UTI — prevalence estimates from primary care populations




                                       90
                       West Visayas State University
           COLLEGE OF INFORMATION AND COMMUNICATIONS TECHNOLOGY
                       La Paz, Iloilo City, Philippines

 • Basic renal impairment indicators — epidemiological

estimates relevant to the demographic context


    These priors are fixed during system initialization

and remain constant during individual screenings. They

serve as baseline risk anchors rather than dynamic

predictors.


Likelihood — P(E|H): The likelihood represents the

probability of observing the collected evidence given the

presence (or absence) of the disease.


Evidence variables include:


 • Visual pad results (derived from k-NN probabilities)


 • Symptom inputs (e.g., dysuria, urgency, fever)


    Each dipstick parameter is parameterized using

clinically reported sensitivity and specificity values. For

example:


• Nitrite — high specificity for UTI, contributing strong

positive likelihood ratio


• Leukocyte esterase — higher sensitivity but lower

specificity, contributing complementary diagnostic weight

                      La Paz, Iloilo City, Philippines

    Symptom variables are incorporated as binary

indicators with conditional probabilities grounded in

documented clinical associations.


Structured Likelihood Computation with Feature Grouping


The combined likelihood is computed as the product of

individual conditional probabilities across independent

evidence variables:


P(E | H) = ∏ P(eᵢ | H)


    However, evidence variables with known clinical

correlation are not treated as independent. Specifically,

variables such as Nitrite and Leukocyte Esterase in the

context of UTI, and overlapping symptoms such as Dysuria

and Urgency, are managed via Feature Grouping. Correlated

evidence is merged into joint probability nodes prior to

likelihood multiplication. This prevents the double-

counting of correlated evidence, mitigating the risk of

artificially inflated posterior probabilities and ensuring

a more conservative, clinically reliable risk estimation.


    Posterior Risk Stratification: The computed posterior

probability P(H | E) is mapped into discrete screening

categories:

• Low Risk

• Moderate Risk

• High Risk

screening results are interpretable for non-clinical users

System Development Life Cycle


    To systematically develop, calibrate, and evaluate the

offline Clinical Decision Support System (CDSS), this study

adopts the Prototyping System Development Life Cycle

(SDLC). This iterative methodology is specifically selected

because the formulation of the system's reasoning layer

(Bayesian Late Fusion) is strictly dependent on the

empirical data and performance metrics derived during the

calibration of the perception layer (k-NN visual



                                   126
                      West Visayas State University
          COLLEGE OF INFORMATION AND COMMUNICATIONS TECHNOLOGY
                      La Paz, Iloilo City, Philippines

classification). Continuous refinement through successive

prototyping ensures that algorithmic constraints—such as

illumination robustness and edge-computing efficiency—are

rigorously met before human sample validation.


    The development process is structured into five

iterative phases :


Phase 1: Requirements Gathering and Architectural Bounding


    During this initial phase, the operational boundaries

and technical constraints of the system are defined. This

includes restricting the deployment architecture to an

offline, Android-based edge-computing environment to ensure

viability in geographically isolated Rural Health Units

(RHUs). The illumination-adaptive parameters are bounded to

a predefined color temperature range of 2700K (Warm White)

to 5500K (Cool Daylight). Furthermore, the specific

clinical targets for the Bayesian inference engine are

restricted to Urinary Tract Infection (UTI) and predefined

renal impairment indicators.


Phase 2: Initial Prototype Construction


    The first working prototype focuses purely on the

computer vision pipeline. The Android application is



                                   127
                       West Visayas State University
           COLLEGE OF INFORMATION AND COMMUNICATIONS TECHNOLOGY
                       La Paz, Iloilo City, Philippines

programmed to capture dipstick images and execute the

sequence of local image processing algorithms. This phase

involves coding the Adaptive White-Balance (AWB) using the

Reference-Based Gray World Assumption targeting the

unreacted white plastic of the strip as the static

reference followed by a marker-anchored brute-force grid

search for pad localization. The prototype is engineered to

successfully extract mean HSV (Hue, Saturation, Value)

feature vectors from the 10 chemically treated reagent

pads.


Phase 3: Iterative Calibration and Model Training


  •   This phase represents the core of the prototyping

      cycle, operating within a controlled laboratory

      environment. The k-NN classifier is trained on human

      urine specimens collected from healthy volunteers and

      processed by licensed medical technologists at the

      partner RHU, consistent with the data acquisition

      framework described.

  •   Images are continuously captured across the three

      predefined lighting conditions (2700K, 4000K, 5500K)

      for each volunteer specimen to generate a labeled

      colorimetric reference dataset.

           COLLEGE OF INFORMATION AND COMMUNICATIONS TECHNOLOGY
                       La Paz, Iloilo City, Philippines

  •   The k-Nearest Neighbors (k-NN) algorithm is trained on

      the extracted HSV vectors, with ground-truth labels

      assigned by the licensed medical technologist at the

      partner facility.

  •   If the classifier exhibits instability due to residual

      lighting noise, the prototype returns to Phase 2 for

      refinement of the AWB gain parameters and pad grid

      search parameters. This loop continues until

      colorimetric separability and visual probability

      outputs are stabilized.


Phase 4: Prototype Refinement and Bayesian Integration


      Once the perception layer consistently outputs

reliable soft visual probabilities, the prototype is

expanded to include the Reasoning Layer. The clinical

metadata interface is built, allowing the input of binary

patient symptoms (e.g., dysuria, urgency, fever). The

Bayesian Late Fusion engine is integrated directly onto the

device, parameterized with fixed disease prevalence priors

and established clinical likelihoods (e.g., the high

specificity of the Nitrite parameter for UTI). The

continuous posterior probabilities generated by the Bayes

formulation are then mapped to the discrete risk

stratification thresholds (Low, Moderate, High Risk).

Phase 5: System Deployment and Clinical Validation


    The finalized, fully functional offline prototype is

deployed for real-world validation in coordination with a

partner healthcare facility. To establish a robust clinical

ground truth in the absence of automated laboratory

analyzers, the manual interpretation of the human urine

samples during Phase 2 will not rely on a single reader.

Instead, the study utilizes an Expert Consensus Panel. The

ground truth for each real-world urine sample will be

determined by the majority consensus of at least two

independent, licensed Medical Technologists evaluating the

dipstick under standardized, controlled lighting. This

consensus approach significantly reduces the subjective

inter-reader variability inherent to visual inspection,

providing a mathematically stronger baseline for evaluating

the system's accuracy and agreement. In this phase,

algorithmic calibration ceases, and the system is evaluated

strictly as a static screening tool. A recognized

limitation of standard urinalysis is the subjectivity and

high inter-reader variability of manual dipstick

interpretation. Therefore, comparing the system's output

against a single medical technologist's visual reading

introduces a circular validation fallacy.



                                   130
                       West Visayas State University
          COLLEGE OF INFORMATION AND COMMUNICATIONS TECHNOLOGY
                      La Paz, Iloilo City, Philippines

    To establish a rigorous clinical ground truth without

access to automated laboratory analyzers (such as the

CLINITEK Novus), this study utilizes a Dual Expert

Consensus as a surrogate gold standard. During the Phase 2

clinical trials, the baseline classification for every

human urine sample will be determined by the blinded,

independent evaluation of two licensed Medical

Technologists. To establish the definitive ground truth, a

100% agreement must be reached between both professionals.

In instances of discordant readings between the two

technologists, a joint re-evaluation of the dipstick will

be conducted to achieve a final consensus; if consensus

cannot be reached, the sample will be excluded from the

final validation dataset. The system's computer vision

output will then be statistically compared against this

strict, dual-verified ground truth using Cohen’s Kappa

coefficient to measure inter-rater reliability, ensuring a

mathematically robust evaluation of the software's

diagnostic accuracy.


    Evaluation metrics, including Cohen’s Kappa

coefficient, Sensitivity, Specificity, and device

computational efficiency (in milliseconds), are calculated

to determine the success of the prototype.

Agreement Evaluation Metrics


  System outputs are compared against medical technologist

interpretations using the following metrics:


Sensitivity and Specificity

                      West Visayas State University
          COLLEGE OF INFORMATION AND COMMUNICATIONS TECHNOLOGY
                      La Paz, Iloilo City, Philippines

Sensitivity and specificity are computed per dipstick

  parameter and per disease risk category.


 • Sensitivity — proportion of manually identified positive

  cases correctly identified by the system

   • Specificity — proportion of manually identified

  negative cases correctly identified by the system


  These metrics are interpreted strictly as measures of

directional agreement with manual reading, not as

laboratory-standard diagnostic validation.


Overall Accuracy


  Overall accuracy is reported as the proportion of

instances in which the system classification agrees with

the manual interpretation across all evaluated parameters.


  Because accuracy does not account for chance agreement,

it is interpreted as a supplementary descriptive metric

rather than a primary agreement measure.


Diagnostic Performance Metrics


  Due to the critical nature of clinical decision support,

overall system accuracy is an insufficient metric. The

system's diagnostic validity will be evaluated using a




                                   134
                      West Visayas State University
          COLLEGE OF INFORMATION AND COMMUNICATIONS TECHNOLOGY
                      La Paz, Iloilo City, Philippines

standard clinical confusion matrix. The primary metrics

extracted will include Sensitivity (the true positive rate,

measuring the system's ability to correctly identify

pathological analyte presence) and Specificity (the true

negative rate, measuring the ability to correctly identify

healthy samples). Additionally, the Positive Predictive

Value (PPV) and Negative Predictive Value (NPV) will be

calculated to assess the post-test probability of the

system's outputs, ensuring the k-NN classifier provides

statistically safe triage recommendations.


Cohen’s Kappa Coefficient (κ)


  Cohen’s Kappa is used as the primary concordance metric,

as it adjusts for agreement occurring by chance and is more

appropriate than raw accuracy for categorical and ordinal

classification comparison.


Interpretive thresholds follow commonly cited frameworks:


• 0.61 – 0.80 — Substantial agreement


• > 0.80 — Near-perfect agreement


  Kappa is emphasized because the objective of validation

is inter-method agreement rather than predictive modeling

performance.

Cross-Device Generalization Protocol


  To substantiate the claim that the system functions as a

generalized 'Smartphone-Based' application, the validation

protocol incorporates a Cross-Device Generalization Phase.

Because different smartphone camera sensors and Image

Signal Processors (ISPs) exhibit varying colorimetric

responses, validating on a single device is insufficient.

across three distinct mid-range Android devices

Samsung, Xiaomi, and Oppo). System accuracy and precision

device evaluation will empirically demonstrate the AWB

for widespread deployment in Resource-Constrained Health

Units.

            Results Interpretation and Analysis


  This section presents the findings obtained during the

implementation, testing, and evaluation of the current

Uritect mobile application. The discussion focuses on the

observed behavior of the implemented HSV-based image

interpretation workflow, semiquantitative analyte matching,

symptom-assisted screening logic, and practical findings

identified throughout testing and experimentation.


  The presented findings reflect the current

implementation-stage evaluation results obtained during

ongoing system refinement and dataset expansion. The

implemented workflow is intended to support preliminary

urinalysis screening and interpretation assistance within a

mobile environment and is not intended to function as a

standalone diagnostic system.


Image Interpretation and Feature Extraction Findings


  The implemented Uritect workflow demonstrated the

practical feasibility of performing smartphone-assisted

semiquantitative urinalysis interpretation using a

Flutter/Dart-based mobile application environment. The

current implementation utilizes single-image capture, HSV-

based feature extraction, k-Nearest Neighbors (k-NN)


                                   171
                      West Visayas State University
          COLLEGE OF INFORMATION AND COMMUNICATIONS TECHNOLOGY
                      La Paz, Iloilo City, Philippines

analyte matching, and symptom-assisted Bayesian fusion to

generate assessment support interpretation outputs.


  During testing, image quality and environmental lighting

conditions significantly affected analyte interpretation

consistency. Variations in smartphone exposure behavior,

mixed indoor lighting conditions, glare reflections, and

inconsistent strip positioning occasionally altered analyte

coloration and affected HSV feature consistency during

interpretation.


  The implemented HSV-based interpretation workflow

demonstrated practical suitability for reagent-strip

analysis because urine dipstick interpretation

fundamentally relies on analyte color variation and gradual

semiquantitative transitions between reaction levels. HSV

representation was selected because it separates hue and

saturation information from value or brightness

information, making it more suitable than direct RGB values

for colorimetric reagent-strip interpretation under

variable illumination conditions.


  The current implementation processes all ten reagent-

strip analytes during interpretation, including

Leukocytes/Leukocyte Esterase, Nitrite, Urobilinogen,



                                   172
                      West Visayas State University
          COLLEGE OF INFORMATION AND COMMUNICATIONS TECHNOLOGY
                      La Paz, Iloilo City, Philippines

Protein, pH, Blood, Specific Gravity, Ketone, Bilirubin,

and Glucose. However, the symptom-assisted screening and

Bayesian fusion workflow prioritizes four clinically

relevant analytes — Leukocytes/Leukocyte Esterase, Nitrite,

Protein, and Glucose — because of their direct relevance to

urinary tract infection and renal-risk screening objectives

established in the study.


  The implemented workflow additionally integrates symptom-

assisted screening logic within the mobile urinalysis

environment. Bayesian fusion combines semiquantitative

analyte interpretation with user-provided symptom

information to support risk-oriented screening outputs

within the Overall Results workflow.

                  System Evaluation Results


  The current Uritect implementation remains under

iterative refinement and ongoing dataset expansion;

therefore, comprehensive stakeholder-based system

evaluation has not yet been fully conducted at the time of

writing. The present implementation primarily focuses on

validating the functionality of the mobile workflow,

analyte interpretation pipeline, symptom-assisted screening

logic, and semiquantitative interpretation behavior during

development and controlled testing.


  Despite the absence of a full-scale deployment

evaluation, the study identified ISO/IEC 25010 as the

intended software-quality evaluation framework for future

system assessment. The planned evaluation will focus on

software characteristics relevant to mobile healthcare

screening applications, including functional suitability,

usability, reliability, performance efficiency,

compatibility, and portability.


  The intended evaluation process will involve stakeholder-

based assessment using structured evaluation questionnaires

and Likert-scale scoring methods. Planned evaluators may

include Medical Technology students, healthcare-related

evaluators to assess both the usability and operational

usability of the Uritect workflow, including dipstick image

acquisition, semiquantitative result presentation, symptom-

assisted screening interaction, scan-history management,

and overall mobile user experience. Additional evaluation
